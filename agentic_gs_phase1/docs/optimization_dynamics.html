Agentic3DGS · optimization inspection

How the optimization evolves — long horizon

A view inside a single stop-disabled v8 agent episode run to the full 30k reference depth, versus the fixed 3DGS schedule, reconstructed from per-block telemetry (Gaussian count, densification and pruning events, held-out PSNR, wall-clock, and the reward decomposition). The aim is to show mechanistically why the learned control reaches near-reference quality in less wall-clock time and at roughly half the model size, all the way to 30k. The scene selector includes truck · real — a real Tanks&Temples capture the synthetic-trained policy was run on zero-shot, where the same dynamics hold (and the compactness gap is even larger: ~1/5 the baseline's count).

Gaussian count over training

How the primitive population evolves over the full 30k run. The agent grows early then switches densification off, holding the count on a plateau; the fixed schedule keeps densifying to ~2×.

Densification and pruning per block (agent)

Primitives added (up) and pruned (down) in each agent block. Densification is front-loaded into the first ~3000 iters (the shaping phase), then switches off — the late blocks only prune, steadily trimming the population toward a compact, opaque set.

Panel C · outcome

Held-out PSNR over wall-clock time

The acceleration itself: the agent curve sits to the left, reaching each quality level in less training time; run long, it converges to near the baseline's ceiling well before the baseline does.

Panel D · mechanism

Marginal efficiency — dB gained per second

Per-block quality gain divided by the block's wall-clock cost (\(\Delta\)PSNR\(/\Delta t\)). This is the rate that acceleration optimizes: the agent extracts more dB per second, especially early, because each iteration is cheaper (fewer Gaussians, no reset recovery) yet still productive.

Panel E · training signal

What the policy is rewarded for (agent, per block)

The block-reward decomposition during the episode: the time-discounted quality gain (green) is the driver; the time, count-excess, and growth penalties (red) price wall-clock and over-budget capacity. The shape explains the learned behavior — large early quality credit, then diminishing credit against a steady time cost, which is what makes stopping optimal.

Reading the panels

Why the agent's choices accelerate

1 · Shape early, then freeze the support (Panels A, B)

Densification is concentrated in the first ~3000 iters (Panel B), while the approximation error is largest and each primitive removes the most loss. After that the agent switches densification off and the count plateaus (Panel A) — the optimizer then descends on a fixed support, a stationary, faster-converging problem, all the way to 30k.

2 · A cheaper iteration (Panels A, D)

By holding the count well below the fixed schedule's (which keeps growing past 2× the agent's), every render and gradient step costs less, so each iteration takes less wall-clock. Panel D shows this as a higher dB/s, concentrated early where it matters for time-to-target.

3 · Refine in place, not by growing (Panels A, C)

Past the densification cutoff the agent improves quality without adding capacity — count flat, PSNR still climbing (Panels A, C). The fixed schedule instead keeps densifying to chase the last fraction of a dB, paying in model size and time for little gain.

4 · Near-reference quality at half the size (Panels A, C)

By 30k the agent is within ~0.1 dB of the baseline at roughly half the Gaussians and ~75% the time. The deployed v8 simply stops early and harvests this same efficient curve at its knee, where marginal quality gain (Panel E) no longer pays its time cost.

Median anisotropy (max / min axis)

The long-horizon trade-off: with densification off, the agent's frozen splats elongate to fit detail (log scale).

Opacity: a head start that the baseline usually closes

The agent drives median opacity up fast (hotdog crosses 0.30 by ~it1600; the baseline never quite reaches it). On the synthetic scenes the baseline's opacities converge upward over 30k — ending comparable (hotdog 0.35 vs 0.29, materials 0.37 vs 0.40), so the edge is mainly a speed effect. On the real truck scene it instead persists (0.26 vs 0.10): the baseline over-densifies to 2.4M splats, diluting its median opacity, while the agent's compact set stays opaque.

Transparent clutter cleared, not recycled

The agent's near-transparent fraction spikes during the early shaping phase, then is pruned toward zero. The fixed schedule's opacity resets periodically refill the <0.01 bucket, leaving low-opacity clutter the optimizer keeps re-fitting — wasted work the agent avoids.

Count: the one persistent advantage (~half)

Across all scenes the agent's population stays near half the baseline's at 30k (83k vs 164k, 153k vs 369k, 158k vs 249k). Densification switches off early, so the support is fixed while the baseline keeps growing — the durable structural difference, and the source of the compactness and per-iteration speed.

Anisotropy: the long-horizon trade-off

With no new primitives past the cutoff, the agent's existing splats stretch to cover detail, so median anisotropy grows very large by 30k (well above the baseline's, which keeps adding rounder splats). The compact model represents the scene with fewer, highly anisotropic Gaussians — the structural cost of buying compactness. (At the early ~3000-iter self-stop the agent was instead the rounder of the two; the inversion is a property of the long horizon.)
