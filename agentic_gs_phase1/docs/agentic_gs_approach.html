The idea

Training as a control problem

Stock 3DGS follows a fixed hand-tuned schedule: the densification cadence, gradient/opacity thresholds, learning-rate decay, and stopping iteration are constants, identical for every scene regardless of how it is converging. We replace that schedule with a learned controller. The 3DGS optimizer is left untouched and wrapped in an environment that exposes its internal state and accepts control actions once per block of optimizer iterations.

Acting at block boundaries instead of every iteration keeps the decision rate low enough for stable RL: one episode of \(T\) iterations is roughly \(T/B\) decisions. All quality signals come from a held-out subset of training cameras; dataset test cameras never enter the state or reward—they are used only for reporting and acceleration-aware checkpoint selection.

Formalism

Markov decision process

One training run on one scene is an episode of an MDP \((\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,r,\gamma)\). At block \(k\) the policy observes \(s_k\), samples \(a_k\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_k)\), the environment runs \(B_k\) optimizer iterations, and returns \(s_{k+1}\) and reward \(r_k\). The objective is the standard discounted return:

$$ \max_{\theta}\;\; J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\!\left[\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\gamma^{k}\,r_k\right], \qquad a_k=\big(a_k^{\mathrm{disc}},\,a_k^{\mathrm{cont}}\big)\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_k). $$

Episodes terminate when the iteration budget is exhausted, when the policy emits the stop action, or when a safety limit triggers. The reward is shaped (Section “Reward”) so that the return is an estimator of early area under the quality-versus-wall-clock curve.

Objective shaping

Reward — making “return” mean “acceleration”

Let \(Q_k\) be held-out validation quality (a worst-view–robust blend of per-view \(\mathrm{PSNR}+w_{\mathrm{ssim}}\mathrm{SSIM}\)), and \(\Delta Q_k=Q_k-Q_{k-1}\) the per-block gain. With current count \(N\) and reference budget \(N_{\mathrm{ref}}\) (the initial point-cloud size):

1 · Complexity budget & time value

$$ e_k=\max\!\Big(0,\tfrac{N}{N_{\mathrm{ref}}}-1\Big),\qquad d_k=\frac{1}{1+\kappa\,e_k},\qquad \nu_k=\exp\!\big(-t_k/\tau\big). $$

The key term is \(\nu_k\). A plain quality reward telescopes to \(Q_{\text{final}}-Q_{\text{initial}}\) and is therefore indifferent to when quality is earned—it cannot prefer a faster schedule. Weighting each gain by \(\nu_k=e^{-t_k/\tau}\) (cumulative training seconds \(t_k\), \(\tau=45\,\text{s}\)) makes early progress worth more, so the maximizer is fast early convergence. Early stopping then emerges on its own once the discounted marginal gain drops below the marginal time cost.

2 · Budget-gated growth & compaction

Below budget, growth and pruning are both reward-neutral—the goal is complexity \(\le\) baseline, not minimal count. Only the over-budget portion of a change is priced:

$$ g^{+}_k=\max\!\big(0,\;N_k-\max(N_{k-1},N_{\mathrm{ref}})\big),\qquad g^{-}_k=\max\!\big(0,\;N_{k-1}-\max(N_k,N_{\mathrm{ref}})\big). $$

3 · Block reward

$$ \begin{aligned} r_k=\;&\underbrace{w_q\,\nu_k\big(d_k[\Delta Q_k]^{+}+[\Delta Q_k]^{-}\big)}_{\text{time-discounted quality}} \;-\;w_t\frac{\Delta t_k}{t_{\mathrm{ref}}}\;-\;w_c\,e_k\\[4pt] &-\;w_g\frac{g^{+}_k}{N_{\mathrm{ref}}}\;+\;w_b\frac{g^{-}_k}{N_{\mathrm{ref}}}\mathbb{1}[\Delta Q_k\ge-\epsilon] \;-\;w_v[\mathrm{VRAM}_k-V^{\star}]^{+}\;-\;w_i[-\Delta Q_k]^{+}. \end{aligned} $$

One positive driver plus regularizers. Each \(w_\bullet\!\ge\!0\) is a fixed scalar weight, \([x]^{+}\!=\!\max(0,x)\) and \([x]^{-}\!=\!\min(0,x)\). Acceleration is not optimized directly—it is the consequence of crediting quality early and pricing everything that costs wall-clock or throughput.

\( w_q\,\nu_k\big(d_k[\Delta Q_k]^{+}+[\Delta Q_k]^{-}\big) \)
Time-discounted quality gain — the only positive driver

\(\Delta Q_k = Q_k - Q_{k-1}\) is the held-out quality gained in the block. Positive gains are scaled by the time value \(\nu_k=e^{-t_k/\tau}\) (cumulative training seconds \(t_k\)) and the complexity discount \(d_k\); quality losses are kept at full weight, undiscounted. Why it accelerates: \(\nu_k\) makes a dB earned at \(t=10\)s worth roughly 3× one earned at \(t=60\)s, so the return is the early area under the quality–time curve. The policy maximizes it by reaching a quality level sooner; keeping losses undiscounted blocks the degenerate shortcut of trading quality away to dodge the time penalty.

\( -\,w_t\,\dfrac{\Delta t_k}{t_{\mathrm{ref}}} \)
Compute-time penalty

\(\Delta t_k\) = wall-clock seconds spent in the block, normalized by a reference \(t_{\mathrm{ref}}\). Charges real time. Why: the policy values seconds directly, so it prefers cheaper blocks (shorter, less densification) and—against the quality driver—halts once added time no longer buys quality.

\( -\,w_c\,e_k \)
Over-budget count penalty (stock)

\(e_k=\max(0,\,N/N_{\mathrm{ref}}-1)\) is the fractional excess over the budget \(N_{\mathrm{ref}}\). Why: fewer primitives means cheaper renders and gradients every iteration—higher throughput—so capping count is itself an accelerator, and keeps the model at or below baseline size.

\( -\,w_g\,\dfrac{g^{+}_k}{N_{\mathrm{ref}}} \)
Over-budget growth penalty (flow)

\(g^{+}_k\) = primitives added above budget this block. Where \(e_k\) prices the stock, this prices the act of growing. Why: it deters expensive late densification that would slow every subsequent iteration without earning its keep in quality.

\( +\,w_b\,\dfrac{g^{-}_k}{N_{\mathrm{ref}}}\,\mathbb{1}[\Delta Q_k\ge-\epsilon] \)
Compaction bonus

\(g^{-}_k\) = primitives removed from the over-budget region, credited only when quality holds (drop \(\le\epsilon\)). Why: it rewards shrinking the model when doing so is quality-free, and a smaller model speeds up all later iterations.

\( -\,w_v\,[\mathrm{VRAM}_k-V^{\star}]^{+} \)
VRAM penalty

Peak memory above the target \(V^{\star}\). Why: keeps the run inside the hardware envelope so throughput stays high (no allocator thrash or out-of-memory), an indirect bound on model size.

\( -\,w_i\,[-\Delta Q_k]^{+} \)
Instability penalty

An extra charge proportional to any quality drop. Why: a trajectory that oscillates wastes seconds re-climbing; damping regressions keeps convergence monotone, so no time is spent recovering ground—monotone descent is the fastest path to a target.

Terminal bonus. At episode end an added \(w_{tq}\,\nu_k\,d_k\,(Q_k-Q_0)-w_{tc}\,e_k\) credits net quality over the whole episode—still time- and complexity-discounted—minus the final excess, so the learned stop is the one that delivers high quality fast and small. A numerical-failure penalty guards divergence, and rewards are standardized per scene (running \(z\)-score) before the policy update so scenes of different PSNR scale contribute comparably.

Dynamics, not incentives

Safety rails

The action range is strictly larger than stock 3DGS, which opens a failure mode a fixed schedule never hits—and which reward tuning alone cannot fix, because it lives in the dynamics.

The prune cliff

Stock 3DGS resets opacity to 0.01 and prunes below 0.005, so survivors recover. The policy may pick a prune threshold above the reset value—wiping the model to the safety floor in one block. Validation quality can momentarily rise during the wipe (it removes transparent clutter), so the reward never sees the damage, and a near-random early policy trips it before learning anything.

The two guards

  • Post-reset prune guard: for 300 iterations after any opacity reset, the effective prune threshold is capped at the stock 0.005.
  • Per-event prune cap: a single event may remove at most 25% of the population, so reaching the floor requires sustained pruning the reward can see and penalize.

Plus rails on reset frequency, an absolute Gaussian floor, peak VRAM, and a minimum iteration count before stop is honored.

Learning

Policy optimization & acceleration-aware selection

Actor–critic + PPO

A shared MLP (3×256, GELU) feeds six categorical heads, a diagonal-Gaussian head (learned log-std) for the continuous actions, and a scalar critic. Trained with PPO:

clip 0.2GAE γ=0.99, λ=0.95 adv. normvalue clipgrad-norm clip entropy bonusKL early-stop

Rollouts span 5 diverse training scenes (chair, drums, mic, ficus, ship). Scene diversity is essential—a policy trained only on easy scenes overfits to their capacity profile and transfers poorly.

Why selection must target speed

Selecting checkpoints by training reward or update count reproduced a regression where more training gave worse test PSNR. Instead, every few updates a frozen deterministic probe runs on a held-out scene (neither train nor eval), scored on its test cameras for acceleration:

$$ \text{score}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|}\sum_{c\in\{15,30,60\}\text{s}}\!\mathrm{PSNR}_{\text{test}}(t{=}c). $$

The mean of time-sliced test PSNRs approximates early area-under-curve. With this metric the objective improves monotonically over training — chart below.

Ablation · stop head disabled

Ultimate performance — how far does the controller go?

The policy normally self-stops near 3000 iterations. To probe its ceiling we disable the stop action (--force-full-iterations) and run the same protocol out to the standard 30k reference depth, with the learning-rate schedule scaled to each budget. The agent was trained to stop early, so every iteration past ~3000 is out-of-distribution — yet its learned schedule keeps paying off.

Gaussian count to 30k — the compactness gap widens

The same run, model-size view. The fixed schedule keeps densifying to the 15k cutoff and ends near twice the agent's count, for the same near-reference quality; the controller holds a far smaller, opaque population. Follows the scene and axis selected above.

Dominant up to ~15k iterations

From 1k–15k the agent is strictly better than the fixed schedule on hotdog — higher PSNR, fewer Gaussians, and less wall-clock at every point (e.g. 10k: 36.89 dB / 70k G vs 36.51 / 92k). The learned strategy generalizes well past the 3000-iteration horizon it was trained to stop at.

Converges early, then saturates

Densification cuts off at 15k, so the agent's compact population plateaus there (hotdog 37.26→37.30 dB over 15k–30k). It reaches its ceiling roughly 2.5× sooner in wall-clock than the baseline reaches the same level.

Reference quality at ~half the model

At the 30k asymptote the baseline edges ahead by only 0.07–0.12 dB — but by inflating to nearly twice the Gaussians: hotdog 156k vs 86k, drums 319k vs 150k, materials 239k vs 165k. The agent reaches ~99.7% of reference-quality PSNR at 47–69% the model size and ~75–80% the time.

A faster path at every quality level

With the stop disabled the agent is faster to every reachable target, now including the high end — hotdog 37 dB in 147 s vs the baseline's 221 s (1.51×). The controller is not just an early-stage accelerator; it is a faster, more compact path to near-reference quality.

Out-of-distribution caveat: the policy never trained past a 7k budget, so this is an extrapolation of its schedule. The clean takeaway is that disabling the learned stop does not break it — the agent keeps improving and stays on the efficient side of the speed–size frontier all the way to 30k.

Zero-shot transfer to a real scene — truck (Tanks & Temples)

The four scenes above are synthetic (Blender, 800×800, random init). To probe generalization we ran the same synthetic-trained v8 policy, with no fine-tuning, on a real COLMAP capture (Tanks&Temples truck: 251 photos, 136k SfM-seeded init, real backgrounds) — selectable as truck · real in the chart above. It transfers cleanly: speed-ups of 1.16× / 1.36× / 1.68× at 22 / 24 / 25 dB, and at 30k it reaches within 0.12 dB of the fixed schedule (25.13 vs 25.25) using only ~21% the Gaussians (432k vs 2.02M) in 57% the time. The learned shape-then-freeze schedule fires on the real scene too — densify to ~530k early, prune back to ~430k, then hold — while the baseline balloons to 2M. Compactness is in fact stronger on the real capture (~1/5 the model vs ~1/2 on synthetic).

What the policy learned

The discovered schedule and its effect on convergence

Decoded from the long (stop-disabled) v8 rollout — the full 30k-iteration trace from the Ultimate ablation. Freed to run long, the policy reveals a clean two-phase curriculum: a shaping phase (~0–3000 iters) that grows and positions geometry, then a refinement phase (3000–30000) that switches densification off and converges appearance on the frozen support. The trace is consistent across hotdog (held-out), materials (held-out), and drums (training) — a general optimization strategy, not a per-scene lookup. Each component maps onto a factor of the convergence rate, as analyzed below.

Decoded discrete strategy

A convergence analysis of the long-horizon schedule

Read off the full 30k stop-disabled trace, each learned decision acts on a distinct factor of the optimization's convergence rate — and the schedule self-organizes into a shape phase and a refine phase around ~3000 iterations.

Two-phase curriculum — shape, then refine

The single clearest structure in the trace. Phase 1 (~0–3000): densification default with a high position LR (>1) and low opacity LR — grow and position geometry while the approximation error dominates. Phase 2 (3000–30000): densification off, position LR drops to ~0.45, opacity LR rises above 1 — freeze the support and converge appearance on it. The policy never trained past 7k yet discovers this split on its own.

Densification self-terminates — bounded support

After the shaping phase the densify threshold climbs toward off / \({\sim}2.4\times\), so the model's support stops growing. The optimizer then descends on a fixed parameter set: a stationary problem with bounded per-iteration cost. This is exactly why, under the Ultimate ablation, the count plateaus while PSNR keeps climbing — growth is switched off, refinement is not.

Block conditioning — phase-dependent step sizes

Geometry and appearance are coupled (moving a Gaussian changes every other parameter's pixel support). Early, a high position LR relocates splats while structure is still wrong; late, position LR is damped (~0.45) so geometry is locally stationary and the well-conditioned opacity block is driven harder (LR ~1.3). This is diagonal preconditioning whose schedule switches as the sub-problem changes from placement to refinement.

Opacity-driven compaction — rising prune threshold

The prune-opacity threshold rises \(0.005 \to {\sim}0.019\) (its cap), increasingly culling low-opacity primitives as the model matures. With densification off, this steadily trims the population toward an opaque, compact set — the mechanism behind the ~half-baseline model size at 30k.

Active-set preservation — opacity-only pruning

Pruning is by opacity alone, never by size, throughout. Removing a primitive that still carries gradient is a projection that discards descent progress; opacity pruning targets only near-inactive coordinates whose removal leaves the objective and its gradient essentially unchanged, so the descent trajectory is preserved while the dimension shrinks.

No stop needed — refines in place

With the stop action disabled the controller does not run away: it stops growing (not optimizing). Geometry frozen, no densification past the cutoff, opacity-only pruning — the descent stays on the efficient side of the speed–size frontier all the way to 30k, reaching near-reference quality at ~half the Gaussians. The learned stop (in the deployed v8) simply harvests this curve at its knee.

In aggregate, the long-horizon schedule is a learned two-phase optimizer: place and grow capacity while under-parameterized, then freeze the support and switch the conditioning to converge appearance, trimming faint primitives throughout. It acts on the constant and rate of the convergence bound rather than on the objective — which is why it is a faster, more compact path to the same near-reference optimum, whether stopped early (v8: $1.30$–$1.58\times$ to target) or run to 30k.
